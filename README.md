## What This Does

A writer sends JSON to `POST /submit` with their text and `creator_id`. The route validates the input, gives the text to the local model signal in `detector.py`, then gives the same text to the stylometry signal in `stylometry.py`. `scoring.py::combine_signals` combines those two scores into one 0–1 AI-likelihood score, and `scoring.py::score_to_label` turns that number into a plain-English label. The decision and both signal scores are written to `logs/audit.jsonl`, and the response returns the content ID, guess, confidence score, reader label, and both signal scores. If the writer disagrees, `POST /appeal` records their reasoning and adds an `under_review` event for the same content ID.

---

## Signals and Scoring

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** `detector.py::model_signal` measures how predictable the submitted text is to a local GPT-2 language model using perplexity. Lower perplexity becomes a higher AI-likelihood score.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated text often follows statistically likely word sequences, so a language model may find it easier to predict than more irregular human writing.

**What it can't see:** Predictability is not authorship. Plain human writing, second-language writing, or writing with common vocabulary can also be highly predictable, while ornate AI text can look less predictable.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** `stylometry.py::style_signal` combines sentence-length spread, vocabulary variety, and expressive punctuation density. Low variation on those measures pushes the score toward AI.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated prose often has more even sentence lengths and more regular vocabulary or punctuation patterns, while human writing can be lumpier and less regular.

**What it can't see:** It cannot understand meaning or authorship. Small edits, typos, sentence breaks, quotations, or punctuation changes can move the score without changing who wrote the text.

### The combining rule

**The rule:** Start with a weighted average. The model signal gets 55% and the style signal gets 45%. If the two signals differ by at least 0.35, the combined score is pulled halfway toward 0.50. That disagreement rule intentionally favors `unsure` over a confident accusation.

**The numbers:** model weight = 0.55, style weight = 0.45, strong-disagreement distance = 0.35, human threshold = 0.30, AI threshold = 0.70.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:** The service is designed so that when one signal strongly says AI and the other strongly says human, the score moves toward 0.50 instead of trusting either one completely. I record both scores in every response and audit entry so the disagreement is visible.

---

## Label Variants

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00 – 0.30 | Our signals suggest this text was probably written by a person. |
| unsure | above 0.30 and below 0.70 | We cannot confidently tell whether this text was written by a person or generated by AI. No accusation should be made from this result. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.70 – 1.00 | Our signals strongly suggest this text may be AI-generated. This is not proof, and the writer can appeal this result. |

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:** The system is not capable of proving authorship. The label says that clearly and avoids turning uncertainty into an accusation.

## Rate Limiting

**My limits:** 10 submissions per minute and 200 submissions per day, keyed by `creator_id`.

**Why I chose them:** A person can revise a few pieces without being blocked, but a script trying hundreds of variations will hit 429 responses. The daily cap slows repeated probing. These limits are local test settings, not a production guarantee.

**What a caller counts as:** A nonblank `creator_id` is the key. Without one, the service uses the caller's address. An attacker can rotate creator IDs, so this is not a strong identity boundary.

**Actual flood test:** `run_attacks.py::run_flood` submitted 15 times as `flood_tester`.

The first 10 succeeded. The remaining five were limited and recorded as rejected entries.

### Diagnoses

**Criterion 3 was MISSED.** All six deliberate inputs returned either `human` or `unsure`, never `ai`. The AI sample scores remained below the 0.70 AI threshold. The stage is `scoring.py::score_to_label`, together with the upstream signal combination: ordinary AI-sounding text does not generate a high enough combined score for this sample set. The label is reachable for other inputs, such as EV10, so this is a missed criterion for the six chosen test inputs, not proof that the AI label is impossible.

**Evasion failures EV01 to EV09:** The stage is the model signal, stylometry, or their combination, as specified in the attack table. Stylistic changes can increase word variety, sentence length spread, punctuation, and perplexity without changing authorship. The signals read those changes as evidence against an AI label.

**Malformed failures MF03 to MF07:** The stage is the route before either model. It accepts control-only, punctuation-only, one-word, emoji-only, and right-to-left text, despite the English-word-based style signal and GPT-2 perplexity being inappropriate for these inputs. The result is unsupported but still returns a normal authorship judgment.

**Other criteria MET:** None of the 10 human practice examples were falsely called AI, the score gap was 0.337, appeals succeeded 5/5, and all five invalid test bodies returned 400 or 413. Each trial repeated the same result. The 10 human examples came from CodePath's staff practice set, not my own writing, so the false-positive finding is limited to that set.

## The Improvement

**What I changed:** I added one input validation check to `app.py::submit`. The route now returns HTTP `422` with `error: unscorable_text` for control characters or fewer than three supported English words. It also records the rejection in the audit log. This stops the service from making an authorship claim about inputs it cannot evaluate reliably.

**Why I changed it:** The before-run treated five unscorable inputs as normal authorship samples. A very long single word received an AI score of 0.8412, emoji-only input received 0.9983, and right-to-left writing received 0.9485. Those are serious false-accusation risks. I chose validation rather than lowering the AI threshold or changing both signals at once.

**What stayed unchanged:** GPT-2, the style signal, weights 0.55/0.45, thresholds 0.30/0.70, labels, rate limits, the original five criteria, and all test scenarios.

## What's Still Broken

**Nine AI-evasion attacks remain broken.** EV01 to EV09 still avoided the high-confidence AI label. The original test specifically required all three labels within six deliberate samples, and that target was not met.

**The detector is still limited.** A non-Latin text with enough embedded English words may pass the new guard. Human-authored text that is too short or in an unsupported script may instead receive a rejection. The current ten human samples are from the staff practice set, not ten original paragraphs I wrote. They give useful evidence, but they do not fully test my personal writing criterion.

I stopped after one input-validation change because the assignment requires a clean before and after comparison. In a separate experiment, I would test more genuine human prose, assess AI evasion by style manipulation, and add language-aware handling before changing signal weights or thresholds.

**The production trade:** I would rather ask for human review than falsely accuse someone of using AI. That protects writers from unsupported labels, but it also creates more review work and lets some edited AI text avoid confident detection.

**What This Extends**

I'm extending Provenance Guard, the local writing-classification service from Units 7 and 8. It already accepts submissions, returns a cautious authorship estimate, records decisions and appeals, and rejects invalid input. This capstone adds request-level observability. I want to know what reached the service, what it returned, and how long the request took. The extension does not change the classifier's verdict.

**The Shape**

I chose **A: Deploy and instrument** with **Path B: local with logs**. The service loads a local GPT-2 model, so I am keeping the working local run path instead of trying to fit it into a small free hosting instance. The new part is structured request telemetry, a reproducible way to measure execution time, and 1 MiB log rotation. I am not retraining the model, changing the thresholds, adding a new detection signal, or building a new interface.

**Running It**

The local startup command and the Python, dependency, and model requirements will be documented in [RUNNING.md](RUNNING.md). This is the same repository as the original service.

**Instrumentation**

`app.py::begin_telemetry` and `app.py::finish_telemetry` wrap each HTTP request. `request_telemetry.py::record_request` writes a UTC timestamp, request ID, method, route, limited input summary (length, digest and preview), response status/result, server duration, and process CPU time. `audit.py` separately records decisions, appeals and rejections. Both JSONL files rotate at 1 MiB per active file, and every archive is kept. These are local single-process files, not a production multi-worker logging system.

**What It Costs**

There is **no API charge** because the GPT-2 model runs locally. `capstone_eval.py` measured **30 successful warm submissions** across three separate trials, excluding model-loading warmups.

- Mean client round trip: **0.128376 seconds**.
- Mean server elapsed time: **0.114047 seconds**.
- Mean recorded process CPU: **0.943229 CPU-seconds**. Process CPU can exceed wall-clock elapsed time when multiple CPU threads work concurrently.
- At **100 requests/day**, the same measured averages imply **12.838 seconds** of sequential client waiting and **94.323 total process CPU-seconds**. The arithmetic is mean × 100. This is not a hosting price or a guaranteed throughput.

The mean is for repeated short synthetic submissions on this Windows machine, not internet deployment latency.

The AFTER run measured another **30 warm submissions**. Its mean client round trip was **0.130634 s**, its mean server duration was **0.113908 s**, and its mean CPU cost was **0.960938 CPU-seconds**. At 100 requests/day, those AFTER measurements correspond to **13.063 seconds** of sequential client waiting and **96.094 process CPU-seconds**. The client mean changed by **0.002258 seconds**. This small variation does not show that log lookup optimization improved model-scoring latency, because the change targets archive reads instead.

**The Improvement**

**Why I chose it:** All five baseline criteria were MET, but the 1 MiB rotation system keeps every archive. The original `log_rotation.py::read_jsonl` implementation retrieved the latest 50 entries using `list(deque(iter_jsonl(...), maxlen=50))`. The result was small, but the function still parsed every archived record. As the retained history grows, that adds unnecessary disk work.

**Exactly one functional improvement:** Commit `b2e55a4` updated the bounded-log reader to search the current file and older archives in reverse order, stopping when it has enough valid records. The returned rows remain in chronological order. Full-history reads, model scores, logging fields, rate limits, 1 MiB archive size, and all five acceptance criteria stayed unchanged. The new regression test covers the latest 1, 5, 20, 50 and 200 records, including a damaged JSON line.

**Controlled benchmark of old and new lookup algorithms:** `capstone_tail_benchmark.py` wrote **1200 synthetic records** to a temporary rotated history spanning **11 files**. It ran the exact original lookup algorithm and the optimized lookup ten times each on that same fixture, checked the latest 50 records matched, and reported:

| Measurement | Original method | Improved method |
|---|---:|---:|
| Median duration (10 runs) | 23.54645 ms | 2.1129 ms |
| Returned last 50 records correctly | Yes | Yes |
| Observed speedup | | **11.144×** |

The full timing arrays are in `results/capstone_tail_benchmark_after_2026-11-17_061231.json`. This is a controlled reference comparison run after the improvement, **not** a claim that the old checkout was benchmarked during the original baseline session. The individual-file 1 MiB limit was also respected in the fixture.

**What's Still Broken**

No original criterion was missed in the BEFORE run. The two-second response-time target was lenient for these short, warm local requests. A future capstone could tighten it to a 0.5-second mean on the same inputs, but I did **not** change it after seeing the baseline.

The change to recent-history reading does not solve the cost of full-history audit searches, nor does it cap **total disk space**. Archived files are retained as requested and will need a storage and retention policy for real usage. The service uses a single local process and an in-memory rate limiter; it is not safe to treat this as a multi-worker production deployment. GPT-2 authorship detection still has the AI-evasion limitations measured in Unit 8.

I stopped at one code improvement because Unit 9 asks for one measured change. If I had another unit, I would address bounded archival search for appeals next, while keeping authors able to appeal old decisions.
